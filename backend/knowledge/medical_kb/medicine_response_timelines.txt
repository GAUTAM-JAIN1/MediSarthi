MEDICINE RESPONSE TIMELINES — MediSarthi shared reference (medical_kb)
Medicine Effectiveness feature, phase 2 (see decisions.md D29)

Purpose: expected pace of response per medicine/test pair, so the app can
tell the difference between "not working" and "too early to judge." This is
hand-curated general clinical knowledge, honestly scoped the same way as the
project's other shared reference files (drug_interactions.txt,
lab_reference_ranges.txt, symptom_red_flags.txt) — not exhaustive, not a
substitute for a doctor's judgement, and deliberately conservative: where a
precise window isn't well established, that is stated rather than invented.

Coverage matches medicine_test_map.py's MEDICINE_TEST_MAP keys exactly, so
every medicine phase 1 can annotate a chart for also has a phase 2 pace
reference. If medicine_test_map.py gains a new medicine later, add its entry
here in the same session — the two files are meant to stay in sync.

======================================================================
DIABETES
======================================================================

Metformin
  - Fasting Blood Sugar: typically shows measurable improvement within
    1-2 weeks of starting or of a dose change. If FBS is still clearly
    elevated after 4 weeks at a stable dose, that's worth flagging.
  - Postprandial Blood Sugar: similar to FBS, 1-2 weeks for an initial
    response, may take up to 4 weeks to fully settle at a new dose.
  - HbA1c: because HbA1c reflects a rolling ~3-month average of blood
    sugar, it CANNOT meaningfully change in less than 6-8 weeks even if
    daily sugars have already improved. Judging HbA1c before 8-12 weeks
    since starting/changing dose is premature — this is the single most
    common false "not working" read for this medicine.

Glimepiride
  - Fasting Blood Sugar / Postprandial Blood Sugar: onset is fast (days
    to 1 week) since it directly stimulates insulin release; expect an
    early, visible effect on daily sugars.
  - HbA1c: same 8-12 week rolling-average constraint as Metformin above —
    fast daily-sugar response does not mean HbA1c should move any sooner.

Gliclazide
  - Fasting Blood Sugar / Postprandial Blood Sugar: 1-2 weeks for initial
    response, similar mechanism/class to Glimepiride.
  - HbA1c: 8-12 weeks, same rolling-average reasoning as above.

Sitagliptin
  - Fasting Blood Sugar: gentler onset than sulfonylureas — meaningful
    change usually takes 2-4 weeks.
  - HbA1c: 8-12 weeks, same rolling-average constraint as other diabetes
    medicines above.

Insulin
  - Fasting Blood Sugar / Postprandial Blood Sugar: fastest-acting of the
    diabetes medicines in this list — effect on daily sugar readings is
    typically visible within days of a correct dose being reached; dose
    titration itself (finding the right dose) commonly takes 1-2 weeks
    of adjustment.
  - HbA1c: still 8-12 weeks minimum to reflect a dose change, same
    rolling-average reasoning as above, regardless of how fast daily
    sugars respond.

Empagliflozin
  - Fasting Blood Sugar: gradual onset, typically 2-4 weeks for a clear
    trend.
  - HbA1c: 8-12 weeks, same rolling-average constraint as above.

======================================================================
THYROID
======================================================================

Levothyroxine
  - TSH: the single slowest-responding test/medicine pair in this whole
    reference. TSH is not expected to meaningfully change for at least
    6-8 weeks after starting or changing dose — this is a deliberate,
    well-established clinical convention (repeat TSH testing sooner than
    6 weeks is generally considered too early to be informative). Do not
    treat an unchanged TSH before 6 weeks as "not working."
  - Free T3 / Free T4: these can shift somewhat sooner than TSH (often
    within 2-4 weeks), but the definitive read on dose adequacy still
    comes from TSH at the 6-8 week mark — an early T3/T4 move without a
    corresponding TSH move yet is expected, not concerning on its own.

Carbimazole
  - TSH: used for the opposite direction (overactive thyroid) — response
    is often faster to begin (some early effect within 1-2 weeks) but
    can take 4-8 weeks for TSH/T3/T4 to fully normalize, and dose is
    often adjusted more than once along the way.
  - Free T3 / Free T4: often the first to show visible improvement,
    sometimes within 2-3 weeks, ahead of TSH normalizing.

======================================================================
LIPID / CHOLESTEROL
======================================================================

Atorvastatin
  - Total Cholesterol / LDL Cholesterol: meaningful reduction typically
    visible by 4-6 weeks; the fuller, more stable effect is usually seen
    by 8-12 weeks.
  - HDL Cholesterol: statins have a smaller, more variable effect on HDL
    than on LDL — a flat or only slightly-changed HDL at 6 weeks is not
    unusual and isn't itself a sign the medicine "isn't working" for its
    main (LDL-lowering) purpose.
  - Triglycerides: similar timeline to LDL, 4-6 weeks for initial
    movement, up to 8-12 weeks for the fuller effect.

Rosuvastatin
  - Total Cholesterol / LDL Cholesterol / Triglycerides: same general
    window as Atorvastatin above — 4-6 weeks for initial movement,
    8-12 weeks for the fuller, more stable effect. Considered slightly
    more potent per-mg than Atorvastatin but the response timeline is
    comparable.
  - HDL Cholesterol: same caveat as Atorvastatin — smaller, more variable
    effect, don't judge this as the main marker of response.

Fenofibrate
  - Triglycerides: this is the primary target for this medicine and
    responds relatively quickly — meaningful reduction often visible by
    2-4 weeks, fuller effect by 6-8 weeks.
  - HDL Cholesterol: modest improvement, if any, usually takes longer to
    show (6-8 weeks) and is a secondary effect, not the main purpose.

======================================================================
BLOOD PRESSURE (kidney-panel side effect, not BP itself — see
medicine_test_map.py's own note on why these are listed here)
======================================================================

Amlodipine
  - Creatinine: not expected to move much either way under normal
    circumstances — Amlodipine is not typically nephrotoxic. A test
    ordered here is more of a routine baseline/safety check than an
    "effectiveness" measure; a stable Creatinine over any timeframe is
    the expected (reassuring) result, not evidence of "no effect."

Losartan
  - Creatinine / Blood Urea: Losartan (an ARB) can cause a small, often
    expected initial rise in Creatinine when first started or when the
    dose increases — usually stabilizes within 2-4 weeks. A modest early
    increase is not automatically a bad sign; a large or continuously
    rising Creatinine, or one that hasn't stabilized by 4-6 weeks,
    is what's actually worth flagging to a doctor.

Telmisartan
  - Creatinine / Blood Urea: same ARB-class pattern as Losartan above —
    a small expected initial rise that should stabilize within 2-4 weeks.

Enalapril
  - Creatinine / Blood Urea: ACE inhibitors show a similar pattern to
    ARBs (Losartan/Telmisartan above) — small expected initial rise,
    should stabilize within 2-4 weeks of starting or a dose change.

======================================================================
ANAEMIA / IRON
======================================================================

Iron supplement (ferrous sulphate/ascorbate and equivalents)
  - Hemoglobin: iron repletion is gradual — earliest meaningful Hemoglobin
    rise is usually not before 3-4 weeks, with fuller correction commonly
    taking 8-12 weeks depending on how low the starting level was. Judging
    this before 3-4 weeks is premature.
  - Ferritin / Iron: Ferritin (iron stores) can actually rise before
    Hemoglobin does in some cases, sometimes within 2-3 weeks, since it
    reflects stores being replenished rather than red cell production
    catching up — an early Ferritin improvement without a Hemoglobin
    improvement yet is not a contradiction.

Folic acid
  - Hemoglobin: if folate deficiency is the actual cause of low Hemoglobin,
    response can be relatively quick — improvement sometimes visible within
    2-3 weeks, more clearly by 4-6 weeks. If the underlying cause is not
    folate deficiency, Hemoglobin may not respond to this medicine at all,
    regardless of how long it's taken — this reference cannot tell those
    two cases apart from the data alone.

Vitamin B12 (methylcobalamin/mecobalamin)
  - Vitamin B12: blood level itself typically normalizes relatively fast
    with supplementation/injection, often within 2-4 weeks.
  - Hemoglobin: if B12 deficiency was the actual cause of anaemia, Hemoglobin
    response is slower than the B12 level itself — meaningful improvement
    usually needs 6-8 weeks, similar overall timeline to iron-deficiency
    anaemia above.

Vitamin D
  - Vitamin D: gradual correction — meaningful rise in blood level is
    usually not visible before 6-8 weeks of consistent supplementation,
    and can take 8-12 weeks for more significant deficiencies. This is
    one of the slower-responding tests in this reference; don't expect
    an early change.

======================================================================
LIVER / GOUT
======================================================================

Allopurinol
  - Uric Acid: gradual, dose-dependent lowering — meaningful reduction
    typically takes 2-4 weeks, with the fuller effect often not reached
    until 6-8 weeks, especially if the dose is being titrated upward
    over that period (common practice with this medicine).

Febuxostat
  - Uric Acid: similar overall window to Allopurinol above — initial
    movement within 2-4 weeks, fuller effect by 6-8 weeks.

Silymarin
  - SGPT / SGOT: supportive/hepatoprotective effect is gradual and
    modest — meaningful improvement, if any, is not usually expected
    before 4-6 weeks, and effect size varies considerably by underlying
    cause of the elevated values. This reference is not confident about
    a precise typical window for this medicine — lean toward "too early
    to tell" rather than a firm verdict before 6 weeks.
  - Bilirubin: similar caution as above — gradual and modest at best,
    not confident about a precise typical window; avoid a firm verdict
    before 4-6 weeks.

======================================================================
KIDNEY (specific renal-support medicines)
======================================================================

Sodium bicarbonate
  - Creatinine / Blood Urea: this medicine treats metabolic acidosis
    rather than directly targeting Creatinine/Urea, so its effect on
    these specific values is indirect and this reference is not
    confident about a specific typical response window for this pair —
    default to "too early to tell" rather than a firm on-track/off-track
    call unless the values are clearly and significantly worsening.

======================================================================
GENERAL NOTES FOR THE REASONING MODEL
======================================================================

- "Too early to tell" is always the safer default when less time has
  passed since start_date than the shortest window mentioned above for
  that medicine+test pair, or when only one data point exists since the
  medicine started.
- A test moving in the right direction but slower than the window above
  should be called "Slower than typical, still within expected window" —
  not "Not responding as expected" — as long as the elapsed time since
  start_date hasn't yet exceeded the OUTER edge of the window given.
- "Not responding as expected" should be reserved for cases where the
  elapsed time has clearly passed the outer edge of the expected window
  above with no meaningful improvement, or the value is moving the wrong
  direction entirely.
- Where this file says it isn't confident about a precise window
  (Silymarin, Sodium bicarbonate), do not invent a specific week-count —
  say so honestly in plain_explanation and lean toward "Too early to
  tell" rather than a confident-sounding verdict not backed by this
  reference.
